Designing for unexpected change

Residuality Stress Test

Ask a practical question: if the world around this product changed in a way we did not plan for, what would the business need to do and what would we need to change? Each test connects an outside change to a business response, a concrete product or architecture change, and the affected parts of the productscape.

A practical way to design for unexpected change

Start with the simplest design that works today. Introduce an outside change, describe the new situation it creates for the business, decide how the business would respond, and record the concrete change needed in the product or architecture. Repeat until new situations can often be handled by changes already made.

01

Explore outside changes

Try varied changes in the business environment. For each one, record what would reveal it, the situation it creates, the business response, and the exact product or architecture change.

  • Use changes in customers, markets, regulation, economics, politics, society, and the physical world.
  • If the list only says that servers or databases fail, the business context is missing.
  • Do not score likelihood; first use the change to expose assumptions and weak points.
  • Keep unusual but credible changes long enough to learn from them.
  • Discuss cost only after the design weakness and possible change are understood.
02

Find shared impact

Map each outside change to the productscape items it affects. This shows where strategy, implementation, and organization must change together.

  • Many marks in one row: the change crosses several boundaries and needs coordination.
  • Many marks in one column: that item carries many responsibilities or is central to adaptation.
  • Columns that change together may share an unclear boundary or duplicated responsibility.
  • No marks in a column means “not tested yet,” not “safe.”

Did the design become more adaptable?

After making the changes, test both the original and adapted designs against a fresh set of outside changes. X is the number handled by the original design, Y by the adapted design, and S is the number of new tests.
